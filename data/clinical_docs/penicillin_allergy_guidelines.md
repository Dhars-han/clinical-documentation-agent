# AAAAI Practice Parameter: Evaluation and Management of Penicillin Allergy

**Source Organization:** American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (AAAAI)  
**Publication Date:** 2025-11-20  
**Document ID:** DOC-AAAAI-PENICILLIN-2025  
**Category:** Clinical Practice Parameter  

## The Burden of Unverified Penicillin Allergy Labels
Approximately 10% of patients report a history of penicillin allergy in electronic health records; however, upon formal evaluation and skin testing, over 90% of these patients tolerate penicillins without allergic reactions. Mislabeling patients with penicillin allergy leads to the use of broader-spectrum, costlier, and potentially more toxic alternative antibiotics (e.g., fluoroquinolones, vancomycin), increasing the risk of antibiotic resistance and adverse events.

## Clinical Evaluation of Reported Penicillin Allergy
When evaluating a patient with a documented or reported penicillin allergy:
1. Inquire about the specific reaction timing (childhood vs recent, time elapsed since ingestion).
2. Characterize the reaction phenotype: anaphylaxis, angioedema, urticaria (hives), vs non-allergic symptoms such as nausea, diarrhea, headache, or viral exanthem.
3. Determine whether the reaction was severe cutaneous adverse reaction (SCAR), such as Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) or toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), which represents an absolute contraindication to re-exposure.

## Clinical Discrepancy Protocol: Patient Denial vs Documented Record
When a discrepancy occurs between an electronic health record documentation of penicillin allergy and a patient statement during a clinical consultation reporting "no known drug allergies":
- **Mandatory Safety Rule:** Clinicians must NOT delete or disregard the allergy label without thorough formal confirmation or documented penicillin skin testing/oral challenge.
- A patient stating "I have no allergies" may simply be unaware of an early childhood reaction recorded by parents or past providers.
- Reconcile the discrepancy through clinical review, allergy history confirmation, or referral to an allergist.

## Cephalosporin and Beta-Lactam Cross-Reactivity
Cross-reactivity between penicillins and modern cephalosporins is predominantly determined by shared R1 side chains rather than the core beta-lactam ring. True IgE cross-reactivity is less than 2% for most first-generation cephalosporins and negligible for third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins (e.g., ceftriaxone, cefepime) that have dissimilar side chains.
